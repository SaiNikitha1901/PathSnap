    01

    The problem

    Kushal

    A car records its position with GPS about once every second. GPS is noisy: tall buildings bounce the signal, so each dot can be 5 to 40 metres away from the real road. Sometimes a dot lands on the next street.

    Our job: for every GPS dot, say which road piece the car was really on, and join those pieces into one connected route. This is called map matching.

    Plate 1 · How noise hides the routegreen = real route · blue = GPS dots
    GPS noise
    Say it like this"GPS dots are noisy. We must find which road segment the car was on at every dot, then build a connected route."
    02

    The four files of one trip

    Kushal

    Each trip is a folder with four plain text files. There are no headers and no ids written inside: the row number is the id, counting from 0. Hover or tap a row to see what it means in the little map.

    Plate 2 · A toy trip (same format as the real files)hover a row
    Hover a row in a file below.
    • Arcs have a direction. 0→1 and 1→0 are two different arcs: the same road, opposite directions.
    • The route lists arcs, not dots. Trip 1 has 1,315 GPS dots but only 525 route arcs. A long road gets many dots; a very short road can get zero.
    • Every trip has its own map. Node 5 in trip 1 and node 5 in trip 2 are different places. Partitions: 80 train trips, 10 student-test trips, 10 hidden evaluator trips.
    03

    Cleaning, and turning degrees into metres

    Kushal

    Cleaning: remove rows that are not numbers or are outside the world (|lon| > 180, |lat| > 90), sort by time, drop repeated timestamps. Our data had 0 of each, but we still check and report it.

    Metres: longitude and latitude are angles. One degree of longitude is 111 km at the equator but only about 64 km at 55° north. So we project each trip to UTM, a flat map in metres. The zone comes from the trip's own longitude: zone = floor((lon + 180) / 6) + 1.

    Say it like this"Degrees are angles, not distances. We convert to UTM so that 50 metres really means 50 metres."
    04

    Giving every dot its correct road (the DP)

    Nikitha

    To train a model we need, for every GPS dot, the correct road. The route file gives the roads in order (A, then B, then C) but never says which dot belongs to which road. So we work it out.

    Step 1 · Why "just take the closest road" fails

    Six dots, route A → B → C. Dot 4 is noisy: it sits slightly closer to road A. Move the slider to push dot 4 around and watch what each method says.

    Plate 4a · Closest road vs. never-go-backclosest road
    Dot 4 position
    road Aroad Broad C

    With "closest road", dot 4 says A. The order becomes A, A, B, A, C, C: the car would drive back to a road it already left. The route file says that never happened. So we add one rule: the labels must follow the route order, and never go back. Among all orders that obey the rule, we choose the one with the smallest total distance.

    Step 2 · The table, filled one cell at a time

    Think of the table as a staircase. Rows are dots (top to bottom = time). Columns are roads in route order. A path through the table goes down one row each dot, and may stay in the same column or move right, never left. Moving left would mean going back to an earlier road.

    Each cell answers one question: "If this dot is on this road, what is the cheapest total distance so far?" Press Next step.

    Plate 4b · The DP table
    cell being filledallowed previous cellscheapest one, usedfinal answer path

    Step 3 · Reading the answer

    When the table is full, the smallest number in the last row is the best total. We then walk back up, each time to the cell that cell was built from. That walk is the answer: one road per dot, in route order. A real trip has about 15,000 dots × 500 roads, which is only 7.5 million tiny "add + min" steps: under a second.

    Say it like this"best[dot][road] = this dot's distance to that road + the smallest best[previous dot][same road or an earlier road]. We fill it dot by dot, then back-track from the smallest value in the last row. Allowing only the same or an earlier road keeps the route order."

    Each distance is capped at 100 m, so one crazy GPS jump cannot spoil the whole alignment. The labels are our best reconstruction, not perfect truth: the median dot is about 5 to 8 m from its label road.

    05

    Candidate roads and the spatial index

    Namitha

    Chapter 4 wrote the answer key. This chapter writes the options: for each dot, which roads could it be on? We take every road within 50 m and keep the 10 closest. The model then picks one of these options.

    The options must be found without the route file, because on evaluation day we only have dots and a map. A big map has up to 300,000 roads. Checking all of them for every dot is far too slow, so we use a spatial index (STRtree): roads are grouped into boxes, and we only look inside the boxes near the dot.

    Plate 5 · Click anywhere on the map
    boxes we opencandidates (within 50 m, max 10)
    50 msearch radius
    10max candidates per dot

    0.24%miss rate, student-test

    Candidate miss rate = the share of dots whose correct road is not among the options. No model can get those right, so it is the ceiling for accuracy.

    06

    Features: clues for each option

    Nikitha

    Every (dot, candidate road) pair becomes one row with about 23 numbers. The label is 1 if this road is the dot's answer from Chapter 4, otherwise 0. Only about 1 row in 10 is a 1. Click a candidate to see its clues.

    Plate 6 · Dot 37 and its three options (example values)
    • Distance clues: how far, which rank, how much farther than the closest.
    • Direction clues: the angle between the car's direction and the road's. The only way to separate A→B from B→A, because they lie in the same place. For slow cars we widen the window until the car has moved 20 m (this fixed trip 77).
    • Window clues: the average distance of the 5 (and 11) dots around this one. One noisy dot may be near a side street, but its neighbours are not.
    • Connection clues: does this road touch the closest road of the previous / next dot? Cars do not teleport.
    No leakageNo feature uses the route file. The route is used only for labels and scoring, so the same features can be computed for any new trip.

    How we score a model

    Shobhika

    100 example rows, 10 of them are the correct road (label 1). Compare a lazy model that always says "no" with a real one.

    Plate 8a · Why accuracy fools you
    MetricIn simple words
    PrecisionWhen it says "this is the road", how often is it right?
    RecallOf all the correct roads, how many did it find?
    F1 / Macro F1F1 balances precision and recall. Macro F1 averages F1 of class 1 and class 0, so the rare class counts equally.
    Log lossPunishes confident wrong answers. Lower is better. Matters because the HMM uses the probabilities.
    Top-1 accuracy ★Per GPS dot: is the highest-scoring option the correct road? Our main metric.
    ContinuityShare of consecutive dots whose roads actually connect.
    Route F1Compares the set of roads in our route with the true route, weighted by length.

    Testing fairly: GroupKFold

    Dots from the same trip look almost the same. If some go into training and others into testing, the model is tested on things it has basically seen. That is leakage. GroupKFold keeps every trip whole inside one fold.

    Bayesian tuning with Optuna

    Nikitha

    Hyperparameters are the knobs we set before training (number of leaves, learning rate, number of trees …). Grid search tries every combination; random search tries random ones. Bayesian search (Optuna's TPE) learns from earlier trials which knob settings look good, and tries near those next.

    Plate 9 · 30 real trials, objective = CV Top-1dots = each trial · line = best so far
    CV Top-1Student-test Top-1Test log lossInference
    LightGBM untuned0.8880.9170.1000.82 s
    LightGBM tuned0.8910.9050.1190.53 s
    The honest answerTuning improved the CV score a little but not student-test. Optuna searched on small 20k-dot samples, so it chose a small model (250 trees, 27 leaves) that underfits when trained on all 1.2 million rows. It is faster, though. We report this instead of hiding it.

    Best values: num_leaves 27 · learning_rate 0.030 · n_estimators 250 · min_child_samples 72 · subsample 0.70 · colsample_bytree 0.41 · reg_lambda 0.017 · scale_pos_weight 7.38.

    10

    HMM + Viterbi: making the route connect

    Namitha

    The model looks at one dot at a time, so it can choose a road that the car could never reach from the previous one. The HMM looks at the whole sequence and asks: "Can the car actually drive from this road to the next?"

    • Hidden states: the candidate roads of each dot (we cannot see the true one).
    • Emission: how much the model likes this road for this dot (its probability).
    • Transition: how believable the move from the previous road is. If driving distance ≈ straight-line GPS distance, the move is likely. Unconnected roads get a big penalty. A U-turn onto the reverse arc costs extra.
    • Viterbi finds the best whole path. It is the same staircase idea as Chapter 4: a table, one column per dot, keep the best way to reach each option, then walk back.
    Plate 10a · Six dots, a main road and a side street
    Plate 10b · Real result on the 10 student-test tripslight = classifier · dark = + HMM

    The HMM helped 9 of 10 trips. Trip 7 got worse (90.5% → 88.6%): its slow, parking-style ending has unreliable direction, and the decoder locked onto the wrong direction for a while.

    91.3%classifier alone (Ensemble)
    95.4%with the HMM
    99.9%route continuity
    98.3%route F1

    Gaps and missing candidates: for a bigger time gap the transition becomes more forgiving (β grows with the gap). After 60 s the chain restarts. A dot with no candidate is skipped, and the gap is filled with the shortest path between the roads before and after it.

    Two laptops: the API and the UI

    Shobhika

    Laptop 1 (Nikitha's Mac) runs the FastAPI server: the models, the road data and the hidden correct routes. Laptop 2 runs only the web page. When you press "Reconstruct route", the page sends a request over Wi-Fi; the server runs the whole pipeline, scores it itself, and sends back the predicted route and the scores as JSON. The correct route never leaves Laptop 1.
